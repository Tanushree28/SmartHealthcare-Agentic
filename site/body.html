From raw ECG to 109,446 labelled heartbeats

01

The signal, before and after

Each record is about 30 minutes of two-channel ECG sampled at 360 Hz. Raw traces carry baseline wander — a slow drift from breathing and electrode movement — plus muscle noise. A 0.5–40 Hz Butterworth bandpass removes both.

The filter is applied forwards and backwards (filtfilt), so its net phase shift is zero. That is not a stylistic choice: a one-directional filter delays the signal by an amount that varies with frequency, which would slide every QRS complex away from its annotated position and misalign all 109,446 beat windows by an unknown offset.

02

Every annotation is accounted for

Cardiologists annotated these recordings beat by beat. Not every annotation is a heartbeat — some mark a rhythm change, a moment of poor signal quality, or a waveform landmark. Those are excluded rather than mislabelled, because folding a rhythm marker into a beat class would inject beats into the dataset that never existed.

A beat is also dropped when its 300-sample window would run off the end of the recording. It is dropped, never zero-padded: padding would invent a flat stretch of signal that a model would happily learn from.

03

What each beat type looks like

Every beat is cut to 300 samples — 100 before the R-peak, 200 after — and labelled into the five AAMI classes. Averaging all the beats of one class shows its characteristic shape.

04

The classes are wildly imbalanced

Normal beats are the overwhelming majority. This is why accuracy is a useless headline number here: a model that answers "normal" every single time scores about 86% while catching no arrhythmias at all.

The pipeline measures the imbalance and reports suggested class weights. It does not resample. Rebalancing is a decision that belongs next to the loss function at training time — doing it during preprocessing would destroy the true distribution and make honest evaluation impossible.

Q collapses from 8,039 to 13 between all records and the training split. Almost every Q beat is a pacemaker beat inside records 102, 104, 107 and 217, which the AAMI convention excludes. They are still processed and written to disk — excluded is not discarded.
05

Splitting by patient, never by beat

The single most important rule. If beats from one patient land on both sides of the split, a model learns to recognise the patient rather than the arrhythmia, and reported accuracy becomes meaningless. No function in this codebase accepts a split-unit argument, so beat-level splitting is not merely discouraged — there is no way to ask for it.

Results below use the DS1/DS2 division from de Chazal et al. (2004): 22 patients to train on, 22 completely unseen patients to test on, with the four pacemaker records excluded.

06

How the baseline performs

A Random Forest on six timing features plus the beat shape, tested on 49,690 beats from 22 patients it has never seen.

07

The finding: the data is fine, the model isn't

Supraventricular (S) recall of 0.271 is far below the 0.760 published for this split. That gap could mean two very different things: the preprocessing lost the signal, or the classifier failed to use it. It is the second — and the evidence is unusually clean.

S beats are defined by arriving early, not by looking unusual. The chart below shows each beat's interval from the previous beat, divided by that patient's own average. Normal beats cluster at 1.0. S beats sit well to the left.

A single threshold on this one feature catches 97.2% of S beats. The Random Forest, handed this feature plus 35 others, catches 27.1%. The signal survived preprocessing intact — the model is not reaching for it. The likely cause is recorded: 30 shape features against 6 timing features means a tree is rarely offered the timing feature when it picks a split.

An earlier version was far worse — 6.3% — because the trees were memorising each training patient's individual beat shapes. That works perfectly on patients already seen and transfers to nobody, which is precisely what a patient-separated split is designed to expose. One parameter, chosen on the validation records, fixed it.

Not a medical device. These are research artifacts. Nothing here is clinically validated, diagnostic, or fit for patient care.

Published figures from de Chazal et al. (2004) are quoted as a reference range, not a like-for-like benchmark — that method used two ECG leads to this one, different features, and a different classifier.
